@ch VI | REJECTION SAMPLING: THE AFFINITY PREDICTOR AS A POST-HOC RANKER (TRACK C)

The routes of Chapter V used the predictor through its gradient. A predictor with moderate accuracy might nevertheless be useful in a different role, as a *ranker* of finished molecules. In rejection sampling, or best-of-N selection, a large pool of molecules is generated without guidance and only the molecules with the highest predicted affinity are retained. This mechanism requires no gradient through the predictor, no change to the sampler, and no assumption about the noisy intermediate states. It is a different mechanism class from everything tested in Chapter V, and it had been explicitly left out of the conclusions of that chapter. This chapter tests it with the Stage 0 EGNN as the ranker.

@sec Design

@sub Pools, ranker and evaluation

Fifteen pockets of the 20-pocket set of Section IV.5 were used, with two seeds each, giving 30 *pools*. Each pool consisted of 300 molecules sampled without guidance (λ = 0), reconstructed, scored once by the frozen Stage 0 EGNN (higher score means a stronger predicted binder) and docked once with AutoDock Vina (exhaustiveness 8) and PoseBusters. Between 285 and 300 of the 300 attempted molecules per pool were reconstructed, and no pool failed. The seeds of each pocket were combined, giving pockets of 576 to 600 molecules. Top-k selection, the random subset and the scrambled filter are all post-hoc selections from the same, already docked pool, so no additional generation or docking was necessary for the analysis.

The scope had been reduced during the run, after a review of the time budget: from 20 pockets and 3 seeds (60 pools) to 15 pockets and 2 seeds (30 pools), because a pool cost about 4.6 h after the optimisations of Section III.7 and the run had to fit in the time available for the thesis. Fifteen pockets remain above the number used in the earlier ablations, and two seeds retain a weaker seed-to-seed consistency check. Pools already completed for a third seed of two pockets were excluded from the analysis and not force-included.

@sub Statistics

The primary selection was the top 10% of each pocket's pool by predicted score (sensitivity analyses at 5, 20 and 30%). For each pocket, the following were computed:

(i) the shift of the mean Vina Dock score of the selected molecules against the full pool, with a 95% bootstrap interval (2,000 resamples);
(ii) an empirical one-sided p-value against 2,000 random subsets of the same size;
(iii) the negative control of Section III.6.2, a scrambled filter with 2,000 permutations of the predicted scores, together with a null-consistency check (KS test between the two null distributions), which no pocket failed, so no implementation error of the permutation was indicated;
(iv) a KS test of the selected against the full-pool distribution, corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure across the 15 pockets;
(v) three diagnostics of quality degradation: the shift of the heavy-atom count, the PoseBusters pass rate, and the ligand efficiency, which was KS-tested and corrected in the same way.

The comparison of (iv) and (v) is the selected subset against the pool it was drawn from, not against an independent sample or against the unselected remainder; the two groups therefore overlap by construction (every selected molecule is also a member of the full pool), which is a deliberate choice and not an oversight. It answers the practical question this chapter asks: does applying this ranker to a pool of otherwise identically generated molecules change what a practitioner ends up using, relative to not selecting at all. A two-sample test is not strictly valid on overlapping groups, so these p-values should be read as a description of how far the selected subset's distribution sits from the pool's, not as a textbook-exact significance level. This does not by itself explain the pattern reported below, because the random-subset and scrambled-filter controls of (ii) and (iii) are built from the identical pool-versus-subset structure and show no comparable shift; any bias contributed by the overlap alone would be expected to appear equally in those controls, and it does not.

@sec Results

@sub Raw docking score

The selection improved the raw Vina Dock score in most pockets. The mean shift was −1.31 kcal/mol (median −1.33; range −3.73 to +1.95), and in 14 of 15 pockets the KS test of the full selected-versus-pool distribution was significant after the correction. This is a different quantity from the bootstrap confidence interval of the mean shift reported alongside it in {tab:trackc}, and the two need not agree for every pocket: SQHC is the one case where they do not, with a KS test significant after correction but a mean-shift interval that crosses zero (−0.13, 95% CI −1.22 to +4.66), because a distributional test can detect a shift in shape or spread that a single point estimate of the mean does not resolve; its per-seed shifts (+0.78 and −1.07) were also opposite in sign, which is the more direct evidence that this particular pocket's effect is weak and inconsistent. Read by themselves, these numbers would suggest a usable rejection-sampling signal.

@sub Ligand efficiency, size and validity

The signal disappeared when the outcome was normalised by molecular size ({tab:trackc} and {fig:trackc}). The ligand efficiency of the selected molecules was significantly worse than that of the pool in 14 of 15 pockets (BH-corrected p < 0.0001 in all these; mean shift +0.099 kcal/mol per heavy atom, where positive is worse); the one exception, PNTM_STRAE, had a small improvement (−0.030). The selected molecules were larger by 12.0 heavy atoms on average (median 12.7; range 7.4 to 15.1), and their PoseBusters pass rate was lower by 28.8 percentage points on average (median 31.6; range 5.7 to 52.7; mean pass rate 0.57 for the pool and 0.28 for the selection). This is the signature of Vina hacking [@gao2025]: larger molecules with poses that are often invalid can receive favourable raw scores without a corresponding gain of binding per atom. The raw improvement reflects, at least in part, that the ranker prefers larger molecules, a preference that is consistent with the correlation of 0.72 between the predictor's output and the heavy-atom count (Chapter IV).

@sub Two pockets with the opposite behaviour

In two pockets, RIBB_VIBCH and P2Y12, the top-10% selection was significantly *worse* than a random subset of the same size on the raw score (empirical p_random = 1.0000 for RIBB_VIBCH, that is, none of 2,000 random draws did as poorly). This held at all four values of k tested for RIBB_VIBCH. In these pockets the ranker was anti-correlated with the independently computed docking outcome, and not just uninformative.

@sub Sensitivity to the selection fraction

The raw-score effect was not stable when the selected fraction was varied. Averaged over pockets, the shift of the raw Vina Dock score was −1.38, −1.31, −0.94 and −0.55 kcal/mol at k = 5, 10, 20 and 30%, and the number of pockets with a negative shift fell from 11 to 12, 10 and 10. Individual pockets reversed sign (HDAC8: −1.41 at 5% but +1.01 at 30%; PNTM_STRAE: −2.91 at 20% but +1.38 at 30%). The degradation of ligand efficiency was the more robust finding at all fractions. It should be read as a monotone weakening of the raw effect with increasing k, which is the expected behaviour of a ranker whose first-ranked molecules are the largest.

@sec Post-Hoc Analysis of the Validity Loss

The drop in the PoseBusters pass rate ranged widely across pockets (5.7 to 52.7 points), and it was asked, after seeing this variation, whether it could be explained. Two exploratory hypotheses were examined, both with n = 15 pockets, Spearman correlations and case-resampling bootstrap intervals, none of them pre-registered or corrected for multiple testing.

The pocket size (the number of protein atoms in the 10 Å crop) correlated *negatively* with the drop (Spearman r = −0.59; 95% CI −0.83 to −0.13; Pearson r = −0.54, p = 0.039; between −0.53 and −0.71 under leave-one-out). Larger pockets thus tolerated the selection better, the opposite of the naive expectation. As a plausibility check, the pocket size correlated with the mean size of the unguided molecules (r = +0.72; 95% CI +0.20 to +0.96). A possible reading is that molecules generated in a small, tight pocket already fit closely, so that a shift toward larger molecules has no room and produces clashes, whereas a large pocket can accommodate a somewhat larger molecule. The spread of heavy-atom counts within the pool predicted the drop independently of the pocket size (partial Spearman r = +0.70; 95% CI +0.17 to +0.91), while the diversity of the pool did not (r = +0.24; CI −0.26 to +0.59). An earlier version of this analysis had truncated each pool to its first 300 molecules, that is, to a single seed, and had produced a non-significant partial correlation; the error was found by re-running the saved script on the complete pool, and the corrected result is the one reported here.

These correlations are hypotheses for a future, targeted study, for example a model of pocket volume against clash rate, and not findings. With n = 15 and post-hoc selection of the questions asked, they are at the limit of what can be supported.

@sec Interpretation

Three conclusions are supported. First, the same frozen predictor that failed as a gradient signal also failed as a post-hoc ranker, by a different route: the selection produced a raw-score gain that turned into a loss when normalised by molecular size, and it lowered physical validity substantially. Second, the negative control behaved as designed (the scrambled-filter and random-subset nulls agreed in all pockets), so the failure is not an implementation error of the selection. Third, the mechanism of failure that this chapter documents is specific: the *selection* is dominated by size. It differs from the mechanism diagnosed for the gradient in Chapter V, where the gradient magnitude was not correlated with atom heaviness in the direction that a size shortcut would predict, and was instead concentrated on atoms far from the pocket. Some earlier summaries of this project describe both failures as having the same root cause, molecular size. This is not what the data show. The two failures are compatible with a common upstream cause, a predictor that encodes ligand-side properties and not pocket-specific interaction, but the evidence for that common cause is indirect (Chapter IX).

Two limits should be kept in mind. The ranker was applied to *unguided* pools of the diffusion model, not to a pool with a different composition. A predictor trained with an explicit size control could behave differently as a ranker, and this was not tested; in particular, the combination of the Track A predictor with rejection sampling was considered and not run, because the size correlation of that predictor (0.64) was only slightly lower. Finally, the result concerns *top-10% selection* by one specific ranker with a stated set of pockets and seeds; the significant effects of the raw-score improvement and of the degradation are large and consistent across pockets, whereas per-pocket verdicts, such as those of SQHC_ALIAD and BSD_ASPTE, rest on weaker evidence.

@sec A Positive Control: Reranking by a Genuine, Non-Surrogate Affinity Signal

Everything in this chapter so far has used the Stage 0 EGNN of Chapter IV as the ranker. A question left open at the end of Section VI.4 is whether the Track C failure and the Chapter V gradient failure share a common cause, a predictor that does not read pocket-specific interaction, or whether failure is simply what any selection-based or gradient-based mechanism produces on this generator regardless of the quality of the signal supplied to it. Neither of the two mechanisms examined so far ever varied the *signal* independently of the *mechanism*: both Chapter V and this chapter, up to this point, supplied only the same trained EGNN. This section removes that confound directly, by replacing the EGNN ranker with AutoDock Vina itself, applied to the identical generator, the identical 20-pocket, 3-seed set used for the baseline and affinity-guided ablations of Section IV.5, and evaluated by the identical independent docking pass used throughout this chapter and Chapter IV. Vina was never trained on this project's pK labels, supplies no gradient, and is a genuine, zero-shot measure of pose quality rather than a learned surrogate.

@sub Design

For each of the 60 pocket–seed trials, an unguided pool was oversampled at twice the usual yield (K = 2), reconstructed, and scored once with Vina's *score_only* mode, which has no conformational search and runs in about 0.57 s per molecule. The better-scoring half of each pool was kept. The kept molecules were then evaluated by an independent, full Vina *dock* pass (exhaustiveness 16, identical settings to the baseline and affinity-only conditions of Section IV.5). The number that decides which molecules survive selection (the cheap score-only pass) is therefore never the number later reported as the result (the independent dock pass), which rules out the strongest form of circularity, in which a selection criterion and its own evaluation metric are the same quantity and an improvement becomes definitional rather than informative. It does not, however, make the two measurements independent, and this limit is load-bearing for how the result may be read: both passes evaluate poses with the *same empirical scoring function*, differing in whether a conformational search is run. Any systematic bias of that scoring function, the preference for larger ligands documented in Section II.3.1 above all, is therefore shared by the selector and the evaluator rather than cancelled between them. The design establishes that the harness can detect a change driven by a genuinely informative signal; it does not establish that the selected molecules are better binders by a measurement independent of Vina. Section VI.5.3 reports exactly the size and synthesizability movement that this shared bias predicts, and the interpretation in Section VI.5.4 is framed accordingly. A 6-pocket pilot, stratified by the measured per-pocket wall time of the existing 20-pocket runs, was run first; because the pilot and the full run share an output directory and this project's DONE-marker resumability (Section III.7), the pilot's results were reused, not recomputed, when the run was extended to the full 60 trials.

@sub Result

{tab:rq2} reports the outcome. Reranked molecules reached a significantly better independently computed Vina Dock score than the unguided baseline: −8.068 ± 2.660 kcal/mol against −6.813 ± 10.079 (n = 600 reranked, 597 baseline molecules across the 20 pockets; Wilcoxon, paired by pocket, p = 1.9 × 10⁻⁶; Mann–Whitney p = 3.1 × 10⁻¹⁰). A target-clustered bootstrap of the per-pocket delta (B = 2,000) gave −1.267 kcal/mol, 95% CI [−2.149, −0.740], excluding zero.

@sub The confound, disclosed directly

The reranked molecules are not a free improvement. They are significantly larger (+1.8 heavy atoms on average, p = 0.0002) and significantly less synthetically accessible by both measures used elsewhere in this thesis (SA and RA-score both fall, p < 10⁻⁴), the same size-correlated pattern that undermined the raw-score gain of Section VI.3.2's selection by the EGNN ranker. If this were the whole story, the result would collapse into the same conclusion as the rest of this chapter: a ranker that prefers larger molecules, producing an apparent improvement that a size-normalised metric erases. It is not the whole story, for two reasons visible in the same table. First, ligand efficiency, the metric in this thesis specifically constructed to detect a pure size-inflation artefact, is flat (p = 0.756) rather than degraded, unlike 14 of the 15 pockets of Section VI.3.2. Second, physical validity does not fall; the PoseBusters pass rate holds or rises slightly, the opposite of the pattern that accompanied the EGNN ranker's raw-score gain. The improvement is therefore partly genuine on a per-atom basis and is accompanied by, but not fully reducible to, a shift toward larger molecules; both halves of that sentence are supported by the table and neither should be dropped in summarising it.

@sub Interpretation: what this isolates

This result answers the question left open at the end of Section VI.4. The selection mechanism tested here, best-of-N selection from an oversampled, unguided pool, scored and evaluated by two separate passes of the same docking program, is mechanistically identical to this chapter's earlier procedure; only the *signal* changed, from a trained EGNN surrogate to Vina's own zero-shot score. Reranking by the genuine signal succeeds, on the same generator, the same 20 pockets and the same independent evaluation protocol on which reranking by the trained surrogate failed by over-selecting on size alone. This localises the cause of the Track A, B and C failures toward the *signal* rather than the *mechanism*: it supports F1, the hypothesis that the guidance predictor does not carry usable information about the true binding interface (Section V.4's gradient-field diagnostic and the representation-ladder audit of Chapter IV point the same way), and it weakens F2, a correctly informative signal misapplied by a flawed mechanism, as the explanation for the EGNN-based failures specifically, because the same family of mechanism, best-of-N selection, succeeds once supplied with a signal that is actually informative. This does not rule out F2 for the gradient-guidance routes of Chapter V on its own terms, because Vina is not differentiable and this section never tested a gradient mechanism with the genuine signal; Chapter IX returns to this limit and to the particle-guidance extension (SVDD, paired with a genuine value function rather than a trained surrogate) that this result motivates as future work but that was not run for this thesis.

This section's own novelty should not be overstated. Best-of-N and best-of-K selection applied to diffusion-based structure-based drug design, ranked by a combined QED, SA and Vina score, was proposed independently as a generation technique by BoKDiff [@bokdiff2025], which explicitly describes itself as the first application of this family of techniques to this problem; Reinforcement-learning-based molecule generation guided directly by docking scores has also been reported to succeed in an unrelated, 2-D setting [@thomas2021]. The selection mechanism used in this section is therefore prior art, not a new technique. Its contribution is narrower and different in kind: using that established mechanism as a *diagnostic instrument*, a signal-swapped positive control run on the same generator and pockets as this thesis's own gradient-guidance and rejection-sampling failures, and read against the F1/F2/F3 mechanism framework of Section III.1 in a way the original study does not attempt.
