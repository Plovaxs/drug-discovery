@ch I | INTRODUCTION

@sec Background

Structure-based drug design (SBDD) seeks small molecules that bind a protein of known three-dimensional structure. Classically, candidates are proposed by medicinal chemists or drawn from large compound libraries, placed in the binding pocket by docking programs, and ranked by scoring functions such as the empirical function of AutoDock Vina [@trott2010; @eberhardt2021]. In recent years, deep generative models have been added to this workflow. Pocket-conditioned three-dimensional diffusion models, of which TargetDiff is a representative example [@guan2023], learn the joint distribution of atom types and coordinates of ligands inside a pocket and can produce new molecules directly in the binding site, following the general principle of denoising diffusion probabilistic models [@ho2020].

A generative model that reproduces the training distribution is not, by itself, a drug-design tool: most sampled molecules bind poorly, cannot be synthesized, or are physically implausible. A natural remedy, borrowed from image generation, is guidance. In classifier guidance, the gradient of an auxiliary predictor is used to steer each denoising step toward inputs that the predictor scores highly [@dhariwal2021]. For drug design, the auxiliary predictors are learned models of binding affinity and of synthesizability, and the appeal is obvious: the generator remains fixed, and only a small, cheaply trained predictor must be supplied. This idea motivated the original formulation of this thesis, the *coupled* use of an affinity signal and a synthesizability signal to steer a target-conditional diffusion model.

@sec Problem Statement and Importance

The central question of this thesis is whether learned guidance signals improve generated molecules on independently computed evaluation outcomes, that is, on quantities that a medicinal chemist would recognise as meaningful and that are computed independently of the guidance model itself. The question is important for three reasons. First, published evaluations of generative SBDD methods rely heavily on docking scores, and it has been reported that docking scores can be inflated simply by making molecules larger or by adding particular functional groups, without a corresponding gain in binding [@gao2025; @xu2022]. A guidance signal that raises such a score is therefore not automatically a success. Second, learned affinity predictors are known to be sensitive to data leakage between training and test sets, and to exploit ligand-only shortcuts [@volkov2022; @graber2025; @li2026lp]; a predictor that looks accurate on a leaky benchmark may be useless for a new target. Third, negative and null results about widely used techniques are rarely reported, although they determine where a field should invest its effort.

The problem can therefore be stated precisely: given a frozen, pretrained pocket-conditioned diffusion model and learned predictors of affinity and synthesizability that are accurate on leakage-safe held-out targets, does gradient-based guidance, or post-hoc selection with the same predictors, produce statistically detectable and non-degenerate improvements in independently computed docking score, physical validity, ligand efficiency and synthesizability?

@sec Related Work

Three bodies of work are most closely related. The first concerns diffusion models for SBDD and their guidance or conditioning by affinity or interaction information: interaction-conditioned generators [@zhung2024; @wu2024; @lee2025bind], diffusion models that inject priors derived from affinity networks [@huang2026], a general binding-affinity guidance framework whose classifier-guidance variant applies a gradient-based affinity signal to a diffusion SBDD generator in a plug-and-play fashion and reports up to a 60% improvement in binding affinity [@jian2026badger], and a target-conditioned diffusion model that integrates bond diffusion with affinity and drug-likeness guidance trained jointly with the generator [@hu2025diffgui]. The second concerns learned scoring functions and the reliability of their evaluation, including physics-informed and interaction-explicit architectures [@moon2022; @yang2023gign], leakage-controlled benchmarks [@li2026lp; @graber2025] and critiques of shortcut learning [@volkov2022; @scantlebury2023]. The third concerns the evaluation of generative SBDD methods themselves, where independent work has questioned the reliability of docking scores as a success criterion and the practice of selecting checkpoints on test data [@gao2025]. Chapter II reviews these areas in detail and identifies the specific gap addressed here: to the best of the author's knowledge, no published evaluation of learned guidance for a 3D diffusion SBDD generator, including the gradient-guidance study above, reports a leakage-safe, target-level split together with statistically corrected significance testing, negative controls and the explicit quality-degradation checks used in this thesis.

@sec Solution Employed and Novelty

Rather than proposing a single new guidance method, this thesis employs a *falsification design*. Guidance was implemented on the clean-data estimate of a frozen TargetDiff model, and four mechanistically distinct routes were then tested against the same evaluation harness: in-loop gradient guidance with two predictor architectures and three mechanism variants (Chapters IV and V), non-gradient rejection sampling with the same affinity predictor as a ranker (Chapter VI), and gradient guidance toward synthesizability (Chapter VII). Each route was assigned pre-declared decision bands, corrected for multiple comparisons, and accompanied by quality-degradation checks (ligand efficiency, molecular size and PoseBusters validity) and negative controls. Two diagnostics were used to ask *why* a route failed, not only whether it did.

A fifth route, described in Chapter VIII, is the redesign of the predictor's training target as a residual against the docking score (delta-learning), which was pre-registered, implemented on a resumable training infrastructure with unit tests and a test-set-selection audit, and evaluated with target-clustered statistics. The novelty of the work lies in (i) the leakage-safe, target-level evaluation of predictors used for guidance; (ii) the systematic, statistically disciplined mapping of where gradient guidance and rejection sampling fail; (iii) the mechanistic diagnostics; and (iv) the methodological audits that accompany the results, including the recognition that an earlier generalisation gap was largely a split artefact.

@sec Summary of Results

None of the five routes produced a defensible improvement. Affinity guidance produced no detectable effect on the independently computed docking score at any tested strength, and the magnitude of the predictor's gradient was positively correlated with the distance of an atom from the pocket, not with proximity to binding contacts. Rejection sampling improved raw docking scores in 12 of 15 pockets, but ligand efficiency worsened significantly in 14 of 15, the selected molecules were on average 12 heavy atoms larger, and PoseBusters validity fell by 29 percentage points. Synthesizability guidance gave no supported gain: in the exploratory design the guidance model's own score rose while the independently computed RA-score fell, and after two fixes this divergence was still present in sign but was not statistically confirmed. Finally, the delta-target predictor was significantly worse than simple baselines, because the residual was not learnt better than by a constant. These findings are reported as legitimate scientific outcomes, together with the limits within which they hold.

@sec Organization of the Thesis

Chapter II reviews the literature. Chapter III describes the data, models, guidance mechanism, evaluation metrics, statistical protocol and integrity checks. Chapter IV reports predictor quality, the discovery of the split artefact and the baseline guidance ablations. Chapter V reports the architecture and mechanism routes (Tracks A and B) and the gradient diagnostic. Chapter VI reports rejection sampling (Track C), Chapter VII synthesizability guidance (Track D) and Chapter VIII the delta-learning redesign (Track E). Chapter IX synthesises and discusses the findings across tracks, and Chapter X concludes and outlines future work.
