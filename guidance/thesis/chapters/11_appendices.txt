@appendix B | LITERATURE SEARCH AND VERIFICATION

@sec Search Strategy

The literature was collected in two phases. In the first phase, related work on generative structure-based drug design, guidance of diffusion models, learned scoring functions, docking biases and data leakage was collected while the experiments were designed. In the second phase, before the delta-learning experiment, a structured review of 45 sources was made for the two ideas that motivated it (residual learning against a physics-based baseline, and interaction-typed auxiliary supervision), together with the nearest published combinations. Searches used bibliographic databases and a research-paper search service with queries on delta-learning, multi-task learning with interaction labels, interaction-conditioned diffusion, classifier guidance for molecular generation, and leakage-controlled benchmarks. An independent check of 12 papers, supplied during the work, was used to revise the novelty statement.

@sec Verification and Its Limits

The bibliographic details of the references were verified in different ways. For most entries, the record in PubMed was retrieved and the authors, journal, volume, pages and identifier were compared with the entry. For sources that are not indexed there (conference papers and preprints), the document itself was consulted. Quotations attributed to a source were checked in the text of the source. Statements about what a paper reports are limited to what could be read: where only an abstract was available, the statement is limited to the abstract, and where a paper could not be read in full (PLANET, and the training loss of DiffInt), no statement about its full content is made.

The search has limits that bear on the wording of novelty. It was made with the tools available, it did not include a manual search of Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar, it covers work up to the date of the review, and the absence of a study in the results of these searches is not proof that no such study exists. Consequently the claim of Section VIII.8 is formulated as "we did not find". A similar limit applies to Chapter II as a whole: it is a focused review of the literature closest to the experiments, and not a systematic review.

@appendix E | POST-SYNTHESIS FOLLOW-UP: TESTING THE DIAGNOSED MECHANISM DIRECTLY

This appendix is, in the terms of Section III.1.1, an *Appendix E follow-up*: every analysis below was conducted after Chapter IX's synthesis had already been written and frozen, and none of it was pre-registered. It is not part of the decision sequence of Tracks A to E (Chapters IV to VIII), it was not subject to the standing two-independent-routes rule or to any frozen stopping rule, and no verdict here revises a verdict already reached in Chapters IV to X. It follows this project's general exploratory-then-confirmatory working discipline (Section III.6.1) and the same corrected-statistics standard as the rest of the thesis, but its status is additional evidence for the main synthesis, not a continuation of the main synthesis's own pre-declared design.

@sec Scope and Relation to the Main Synthesis

Chapter IX's "Grading the explanations" (Section IX.2.3) listed four hypotheses that were consistent with the measured evidence but not tested: (a) that the mismatch between clean training poses and noisy intermediate diffusion states makes the gradient unreliable; (b) that the evaluation target (Vina) and the training target (experimental affinity) ask different questions; (c) that position-only guidance, with the atom-type gradient set to zero and no gradient path through the denoiser, is too weak or too blunt an intervention; and (d) that a smooth 3D gradient is a poor way to steer a fingerprint-based synthesizability target. After that synthesis was written, 8 further checkpoints were independently trained and tested to address hypotheses (a) and (c) directly, together with two closely related questions raised by the main result: whether the null pattern survives independent, chemistry-level validation (not just a docking score) and the hardest train/test separation this project can construct, and what happens, mechanistically, to a single molecule's atoms when the gradient is actually applied step by step. This appendix reports that work in full. It changes none of Chapter IX's conclusions; it deepens the evidence for them and resolves, where possible, which of the untested hypotheses the new evidence favours.

The 8 checkpoints are organised in three phases, following the order in which they were run. Phase 1 (E.2) varies the predictor's training recipe and representation directly, taking the diagnostic in Section V.4 as a training target or adding a genuinely new information source. Phase 2 (E.3) tests the resulting null pattern under independent, non-docking validation and under the strictest generalisation split available. Phase 3 (E.4) sharpens one of Phase 1's representations and independently cross-checks the project's affinity labels. Section E.5 reports the mechanistic trace that answers hypothesis (c) directly, at the level of one molecule's atoms, step by step. Section E.6 pools every real-docking comparison of this appendix with the pool of Chapter IX into a single correction. All checkpoints were trained on the same leakage-safe split as Chapter IV (Section III.3), and every real-docking test reuses the KS-test and Wilcoxon-signed-rank protocols of Section III.6.1 unless stated otherwise.

@sub Noise-matched and Vina-derived training recipes

Hypothesis (a) was tested directly: a noise-matched curriculum trains the Stage 0 EGNN predictor on ligand positions corrupted by the same forward-diffusion schedule it will see at inference (t ~ Uniform(0, 500)) instead of on clean docked poses only. A second, independent change (the "Vina-target" recipe) replaces the heterogeneous experimental pK label with a Vina-derived anchor, so the predictor's training target matches the evaluation metric, testing hypothesis (b) at the same time. Both recipes were trained on the EGNN backbone and, together, on the physics-anchored GIGN+PIGNet2 backbone of Track A (Section V.2), to ask whether a null pattern specific to the original training recipe might behave differently under a recipe suggested by the diagnosed mechanism itself.

Neither recipe changed the outcome. On the EGNN backbone, a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test against the unguided baseline (n = 8 molecules per condition, three non-zero λ each) gave no p-value below 0.10 for either recipe (noise-matched: p = 0.156 / 0.250 / 0.312 at λ = 0.1 / 1.0 / 10.0; Vina-target: p = 0.312 / 0.102 / 0.945), with mean shifts no larger than 0.14 kcal/mol in either direction. On GIGN+PIGNet2, both recipes applied together and tested like-for-like against the original Track A protocol (8 pockets, n = 30 molecules per condition, independent Vina Dock and PoseBusters) gave 0/16 comparisons significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction [@benjamini1995] (minimum raw p = 0.031, minimum corrected p = 0.489), a result reported in full in {tab:fupool}. The one notable surface change was the correct-direction rate: 14 of 16 comparisons (87.5%) pointed the expected way, the highest rate of any checkpoint in this investigation, including the original Track A checkpoint's 67%. PoseBusters validity degraded in 9 of 16 conditions (56%), concentrated at the higher of the two tested strengths. The directional improvement did not translate into a single significant, corrected effect; this is the pattern that recurs through the rest of this appendix.

@sub Gradient-alignment: optimising the diagnosed correlation directly

The clearest test of whether the Section V.4 diagnostic identifies a cause, rather than a correlate, is to optimise it directly and see whether a real guidance effect follows. The gradient-alignment checkpoint (`guidance/lp_split/train_egnn_stage0_gradalign.py`) adds an auxiliary training loss that directly minimises the gradient-magnitude-to-pocket-distance correlation during training, rather than changing it as a side effect of some other intervention. It reaches by far the strongest, correctly signed value of this diagnostic of any checkpoint in this investigation: −0.784 (95% CI [−0.799, −0.770]), against the original checkpoint's +0.240. {tab:fudiag} places this against every other checkpoint tested.

This is also the checkpoint with the worst size/heaviness correlation of any variant tried (+0.331), a trade-off the training loss never targeted: fixing the diagnosed distance correlation most strongly of anything attempted made a different, related symptom worse, with nothing in the objective holding it in place. If directly optimising the diagnosed mechanism were sufficient to produce a real guidance effect, this is the checkpoint most likely to show it; instead it produced the flattest real-docking result of the entire investigation.

@sub A global protein-language-model feature (ESM2)

A different kind of intervention — adding a new information source rather than changing the training curriculum, label or loss — concatenates a mean-pooled ESM2 embedding of the target protein's full sequence (model esm2_t12_35M_UR50D, 480 dimensions, projected to 32 before concatenation with the pooled graph representation) [@lin2023esm2] to the Stage 0 EGNN, trained on the Vina-target recipe. This produced the best predictive quality and the best diagnostic of any variant that was not told, by construction, to optimise that diagnostic ({tab:fuquality}, {tab:fudiag}): test R² = 0.460, Pearson = 0.731, Spearman = 0.748, and a distance correlation of −0.072 (95% CI [−0.106, −0.040]) that reaches the correct sign with a CI excluding zero, the only checkpoint besides gradient-alignment itself to do so.

An exploratory check (n = 8, fixed pocket) showed a clean monotonic dose-response unlike anything else in this investigation: Vina Dock improving from −10.46 (λ = 0) to −12.34 (λ = 3.0) with single-fragment rate and validity both at 1.0 throughout, collapsing only at λ = 10 and λ = 30. Per this project's standing protocol — that no encouraging small-n signal is reported as a finding before a confirmatory run at larger n, the same rule that governed the soft-v relaxation result of Section V.2 — this was not reported until confirmed at n = 24. {tab:fudose} gives the result.

@sub Kitchen-sink: all four interventions combined

The one combination not yet tried after eleven independently-trained checkpoints showed the same null pattern stacks all four interventions above (noise-matched curriculum, Vina-target label, gradient-alignment loss, ESM2 feature) in one checkpoint, run to ask whether any pair of them might interact rather than because a specific mechanism predicted synergy. Predictive quality (R² = 0.413, Pearson = 0.664, Spearman = 0.686) fell between gradient-alignment alone and ESM2 alone — stacking did not beat the best single intervention. A visible overfitting signature appeared once four objectives were optimised jointly (validation R² briefly went negative at epoch 3 before the best checkpoint was recovered at epoch 2), not present in any single-intervention checkpoint at the same training budget. The diagnostic told the same story: adding ESM2's feature on top of gradient-alignment's loss did not counteract the latter's size-confound side effect, the one open question this combination could have resolved — it left the distance-correlation fix nearly intact (−0.757, against gradient-alignment alone's −0.784) while making the size/heaviness correlation slightly worse (+0.354, now the worst of any checkpoint). The real-docking test (prelambda check, n = 8, full seven-point grid) showed no encouraging direction at any λ, small and inconsistently signed diffs, worst at the highest strength rather than any dose-response; structural validity, notably, never degraded even out to λ = 30, the only checkpoint in this appendix for which that holds. This closes the ablation space of Phase 1 cleanly: no single intervention, pair, or full combination of the four tried (noise-matching, Vina-target, gradient-alignment, ESM2) produces a real-docking effect that survives correction.

@sec Phase 2: Independent Validation and the Hardest Generalisation Test

@sub Does guidance change real chemistry, not just the raw score? (PLIP)

Every real-docking test to this point asks whether the raw Vina Dock score moves. A different, closely related question is whether guidance changes the number of real, geometrically detected protein–ligand interactions a pose actually forms — hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, π-stacking and salt bridges, counted by the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) [@salentin2015; @adasme2021] on the same Vina-docked poses used throughout this appendix. This matters specifically against one alternative explanation for the overall null pattern: that Vina Dock is a misleading proxy hiding a real, chemistry-level improvement a score-only metric cannot see.

An exploratory tier on the ESM2 checkpoint (n = 8, λ = 3.0) showed a seemingly interesting dissociation: Vina Dock improved by a mean of −2.27 kcal/mol (raw p = 0.031) while PLIP-detected interactions decreased by a mean of −3.71 (p = 0.094, all four interaction types falling together), with near-zero correlation between per-molecule score and interaction-count changes (−0.02 to −0.13) — a "chemically hollow" score gain, on its face. Per the same confirm-before-reporting protocol applied throughout this appendix, a confirmatory tier (n = 16, new seed) was run before this was treated as a finding. The signal did not merely weaken; it reversed sign ({fig:fuplip}): interactions changed from −3.71 to +1.00, and the Vina Dock shift itself weakened to −1.02 kcal/mol (p = 0.077, no longer below 0.05). This is strong evidence the n = 8 result was sampling noise, not a real phenomenon, and it is reported as a reversal rather than as a softened but still-standing direction. A second checkpoint with the opposite diagnostic profile (gradient-alignment, the strongest distance-correlation fix and the flattest real-docking result) showed the same null at both the score and the interaction level (n = 8, λ ∈ {1.0, 10.0}, no comparison significant in either direction). Neither checkpoint tested — the one that came closest to a real docking effect, or the one with the strongest mechanistic fix — shows a defensible chemistry-level guidance effect in either direction. This result is reported on its own terms and is not pooled into Section E.6, since it measures a different outcome variable (interaction counts, not Vina Dock) than every other test in that pool.

@fig fuplip | guidance/followup_figures/fig9_plip_signal_reversal.png | 4.6 | PLIP-detected interaction-count shift under guidance on the ESM2 checkpoint, exploratory tier (n=8) against confirmatory tier (n=16, new seed). The sign of the effect reverses, not merely weakens, between the two tiers.

@sub Generalisation to a protein family never seen in training

The leakage-safe split of Chapter IV separates test targets from training targets by protein identity, but still allows a test target to share a fold or family with something the predictor saw during training. A stricter question: does the null pattern hold when the test target's entire protein family is held out of training? PF00069, the protein kinase domain and the single largest family in the split's own Pfam annotations [@mistry2021], was removed entirely — 53 targets (8,880 entries) moved to test, with no kinase-family target appearing anywhere in training or validation.

Point-prediction quality did not collapse on the unseen family; if anything it was somewhat better than the same-distribution checkpoint's own numbers (R² = 0.507, Pearson = 0.713, Spearman = 0.755, against the familiar-distribution Vina-target checkpoint's 0.400 / 0.652; {tab:fuquality}). The diagnostic was healthier too, and for the first time on a non-forced checkpoint reached significance in the correct direction without any auxiliary loss asking it to: −0.086 (95% CI [−0.136, −0.037]), against the familiar-distribution checkpoint's wrongly signed +0.047 ({tab:fudiag}). The real-docking test, run on CDK6_HUMAN (a major real-world kinase drug target and a member of the held-out family, prelambda check, n = 8, full grid), showed no significant effect at any λ (Wilcoxon p = 0.195 to 0.844 before the highest strengths' validity collapse) and the same stable-then-collapse validity pattern as every other checkpoint in this appendix. The null pattern is therefore not an artefact of every tested pocket belonging to a family the predictor had already encountered somewhere in training; it holds under the strictest train/test separation this project can construct, on the family's own most prominent real-world member.

@fig fufamily | guidance/followup_figures/fig7_family_holdout_quality.png | 4.8 | Test-set predictive quality of the Vina-target recipe evaluated on its ordinary, same-distribution test set against the same recipe trained with the entire kinase (PF00069) family held out and evaluated only on that family. Quality does not collapse on the unseen family.

The headline numbers were remarkably stable from the preliminary n=40 estimate to the full n=835 result ({tab:fuafresult}): a small sample was, in this case, not a biased or lucky one, now confirmed at a dramatically larger scale (correlation p-values moving from the e-8/e-10 range to the e-92/e-117 range). The practical reading is that pLDDT is a good average-case guide to pocket accuracy and not a guarantee: roughly one in 64 "confident" predictions in this sample is substantially wrong at the binding site specifically, a failure mode the confidence score alone cannot flag. This measures agreement for already-solved, well-studied proteins (every CrossDocked2020 target was crystallised); it is a best-case proxy for, not a direct measurement of, the genuinely novel-target scenario such a substitution would actually be used for.

@sec Phase 3: Sharpening the Representation and an Independent Label Check

@sub A pocket-restricted protein-language-model feature

Phase 1's ESM2 feature pools the embedding over the entire protein chain, a coarse signal relative to the handful of residues that actually line the binding site. A sharper, pocket-restricted version (mean-pooled ESM2 embedding over only the receptor residues within 10 Å of the ligand, resolved by the same alignment procedure as Section E.3.3, 847/971 targets, 87% coverage) tests whether sharpening this signal helps either predictive quality or the real-docking outcome.

It did not help either, and on predictive quality it clearly hurt: test R² fell from 0.460 (whole-protein) to 0.280, with Pearson and Spearman both falling slightly as well (0.731 → 0.686; 0.748 → 0.723; {tab:fuquality}). Restricting ESM2's mean-pooling to a handful of pocket residues (typically 3 to 60 per target, far fewer than a full chain of hundreds) produced a noisier, less accurate predictor than pooling over the whole protein — a plausible but unverified explanation is that mean-pooling over so few residues gives a much higher-variance per-target estimate than averaging a full chain. The diagnostic was healthy but did not improve on the whole-protein version: the distance correlation kept the correct sign but lost significance (−0.026, 95% CI [−0.069, 0.017], against the whole-protein version's significant −0.072; {tab:fudiag}). The real-docking test (prelambda check, n = 8, full grid) showed a mean effect that grew monotonically with λ up to 3.0, non-significant throughout (p = 0.148 to 0.945), reaching its closest approach to significance (p = 0.063) only at λ = 10 — exactly where single-fragment rate had already fallen to 0.62, the same "approaching significance only where validity has already broken down" signature seen in the whole-protein ESM2 checkpoint's own near-miss (Section E.2.3). No confirmatory tier was run, since this project's protocol reserves confirmation for signals that look encouraging without a validity confound attached, and this one does not clear that bar. This is also the first checkpoint in this appendix to make predictive quality actively worse than its own predecessor by trying to sharpen a signal, rather than add a new one.

@sec Direct Mechanistic Trace of the Guidance Nudge

Sections E.2 to E.4 establish, over 8 checkpoints and every mechanism-level intervention this project could construct, that the real-docking null is not explained by predictor quality, training recipe, representation, or the difficulty of the generalisation test. This section asks the question the earlier sections cannot: what actually happens, step by step, to one molecule's atoms when the gradient is applied? If hypothesis (c) of Section IX.2.3 is correct — that position-only guidance is too weak or too blunt an intervention on a denoiser that was never trained to expect it — a direct trace of the sampling trajectory should show the mechanism by which the nudge fails to matter, not merely confirm, again, that it does not.

@sub Method

One molecule was sampled twice from bit-identical starting noise and identical per-step stochastic draws (the random seed was reset immediately before each call), once unguided (λ = 0) and once guided (λ = 1.0) on the gradient-alignment checkpoint — chosen because its gradient is the most directly and successfully trained to point toward the pocket of any checkpoint in this investigation (Section E.2.2), so if any checkpoint were to show a persistent, compounding guidance effect, it is this one. Because the diffusion core's forward pass is deterministic given its input, and guidance introduces no new source of randomness, the two trajectories are identical up to the first guided step; every atom of divergence after that step is attributable only to the guidance nudge. New instrumentation (`guided_sampling.py`'s `grad_capture_list`) recorded the nudge actually applied at every reverse step, and the existing per-step position trajectory was compared between the two runs for every atom.

@sub Result: the gap grows smoothly and ends two orders of magnitude below the raw nudge sum

| Step (raw timestep) | Mean all-atom gap (Å) |
| 0 (999, noisiest) | 0.0000 |
| 100 (899) | 0.0006 |
| 400 (599) | 0.0014 |
| 700 (299) | 0.0035 |
| 800 (199) | 0.0085 |
| 900 (99) | 0.0167 |
| 1000 (0, final) | 0.0512 (mean); 0.1314 (max, any single atom) |
@endtab

The gap starts at exactly zero, grows smoothly rather than flat or oscillating, and accelerates sharply only in the final third of sampling ({tab:futrace}, {fig:futracegap}). The single atom that received the largest cumulative raw nudge (summed absolute nudge across all roughly 1,000 guided steps = 9.49, in the guidance model's own gradient units) ends the trajectory only 0.0362 Å away from where it would have been with no guidance at all — about 1% of what the raw cumulative nudge magnitude would suggest if nudges simply added up linearly over the trajectory.

@fig futracegap | guidance/example_render/trace_gap_plot.png | 5.6 | Mean all-atom position gap between the guided (λ=1.0) and unguided (λ=0) trajectory, by reverse-diffusion step, for the paired deterministic trace of Section E.5. The gap is exactly zero until guidance is first applied, grows smoothly, and only accelerates in the final third of sampling.

This is a specific, falsifiable mechanistic account, not merely "the effect is small". At each reverse-diffusion step, the posterior sampling step adds its own intrinsic stochastic noise, scaled by the DDPM schedule's posterior variance, which is large early in sampling (high raw timestep) and shrinks toward the end. Early in sampling, this intrinsic noise is large relative to the fixed-size deterministic guidance nudge, so the nudge is statistically buried in noise the model would have injected regardless of guidance — consistent with the near-zero gap through roughly the first 80% of sampling in {tab:futrace}. Only as the schedule's posterior variance shrinks toward the end does the same-sized nudge's relative contribution grow enough to produce a measurable, compounding effect — consistent with the late-trajectory acceleration observed. The nudge is not actively cancelled by a corrective force at each step; it is mostly drowned out by noise the model would add regardless of guidance, with only a small residual surviving to compound. This directly supports hypothesis (c) of Section IX.2.3 in a specific form: position-only guidance is weak not because its direction is wrong, but because its magnitude is small relative to the sampler's own injected noise for most of the trajectory.

@sub A puzzle checked, not glossed over

Despite the tiny geometric gap (at most 0.13 Å for any atom), the two runs' final reconstructed molecules have completely different SMILES, both single-fragment and both chemically valid ({fig:futracemols}). This was checked rather than assumed away. The most likely explanation is that atom-type sampling (a Gumbel-max argmax over discrete logits, a decision-boundary-sensitive operation by construction) is sensitive to even this small a position-driven perturbation at some intermediate step, after which the coupled position-and-type forward pass diverges far more in the discrete channel than in the continuous one that {tab:futrace} measures — a "butterfly effect" specific to discrete sampling, not evidence that the position-level effect was itself large. The specific step at which the type-sampling decision flips was not isolated, and this detail is flagged as open rather than claimed as settled.

@sub Scope and what would have rejected this hypothesis

This is a mechanistic case study of one molecule, one checkpoint, one guidance strength — it demonstrates a concrete, falsifiable phenomenon and does not by itself make a population-level statistical claim; that evidence is Section E.6's pooled correction. Had the gap instead grown to a large fraction of the raw cumulative nudge (multiple Ångströms, comparable to the summed nudge magnitude of 9.49) while the molecule remained chemically valid, that would have pointed to the guidance direction itself being the problem — a persistent, uncancelled push in a direction that simply does not help Vina Dock — rather than the injection mechanism. That is not what was observed: the gap stays two orders of magnitude below the raw nudge sum throughout, a result consistent specifically with the injection-mechanism hypothesis stated in advance, not merely consistent with "nothing happened".

@sec What This Appendix Adds to the Thesis's Central Claim

Chapter IX's hypotheses (a) and (c) — that the clean/noisy training mismatch, and the weakness of position-only guidance, might explain the null result — were both, at the time that chapter was written, untested and flagged as outside the compute budget available. Both were tested directly in this appendix. Hypothesis (a) does not explain the null: a predictor trained on exactly the noise distribution it will see at inference (Section E.2.1), and a predictor given an entirely new, non-noise-related information source (ESM2, Section E.2.3), both leave the pattern unchanged. Hypothesis (c) is directly supported, in a specific mechanistic form, by the trace of Section E.5: the position-only nudge is small enough, for most of the sampling trajectory, to be statistically buried in the sampler's own intrinsic step noise, and only compounds to a measurable but still sub-Ångström effect in the final steps, where it is large enough to flip discrete atom-type decisions without being large enough to move the docking score in a defensible, reproducible way. The two independent validation branches of Phase 2 (PLIP interaction counts, Section E.3.1; the unseen-protein-family generalisation test, Section E.3.2) rule out two further alternative explanations: that Vina Dock hides a real chemistry-level effect, and that the null pattern is an artefact of every tested pocket belonging to a protein family the predictor had already seen. None of this changes Chapter IX's central conclusion. It replaces two previously untested hypotheses with a tested, mechanistically specific account of why position-only gradient guidance on a frozen, pretrained diffusion model does not produce a defensible improvement under the conditions this project could test, and it rules out the two most plausible ways that conclusion could have been an artefact of the predictor, the evaluation metric, or the generalisation difficulty of the test set.
