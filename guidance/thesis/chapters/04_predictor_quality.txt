@ch IV | AFFINITY PREDICTOR QUALITY, THE SPLIT ARTEFACT AND BASELINE GUIDANCE ABLATIONS

This chapter reports the first phase of the experimental work. It starts with the affinity predictor of the original guidance implementation, shows that its poor held-out quality was investigated through a leakage audit, a hyper-parameter sweep and five diagnostics, and then shows that most of the apparent generalisation gap was caused by the way the data had been split. After the predictor had been repaired in this sense, the first guidance ablations were run, on the original and on the repaired predictor. All of them returned null results, which motivated the routes reported in Chapters V to VIII.

@sec The Original Affinity Predictor

@sub Training set-up

The first guidance predictor was the Stage 0 EGNN architecture of {tab:predictors}, trained to regress the experimental pK of a pocket–ligand pair from the docked pose. Training used the official CrossDocked2020 training partition with a random 90/10 division of pocket instances into training and validation sets, a random subsample of 6,000 training instances (507 targets) because of the training time on a 4 GB GPU, and the 27 labelled pockets of the official test partition as the held-out test set (26 targets, disjoint from training). The label distributions were similar (training 6.76 ± 1.84, validation 6.85 ± 1.89, test 5.71 ± 1.81 pK units), although the test mean was lower. Training was run for at most 20 epochs with early stopping, and the checkpoint of the last epoch, which had the lowest validation loss (1.375), was deployed.

@sub Held-out quality

The validation and test results of the deployed predictor differed strongly ({tab:oldquality}). Validation Pearson correlation rose steadily to 0.79, whereas test Pearson correlation fluctuated between 0.12 and 0.39 with no trend, and test R² was negative at every logged epoch, meaning that the predictions were worse than always predicting the training mean. The deployed checkpoint was not even the best on the test set: the highest test correlation (0.386) was recorded at epoch 3, which was not a running-best validation epoch.

The disagreement between validation and test had a structural explanation that was recognised at this point. Because the validation set was a random division of *pocket instances*, and CrossDocked2020 contains many poses of each target, the validation set shared 74% of its targets with the training set (507 training targets and 456 validation targets drawn from one pool of about 1,900 targets). This 507-target figure is itself a consequence of two compounded subsampling steps and not of the instance-level 90/10 division alone: the 90/10 split was drawn across the full training partition (99,990 instances, 1,905 targets), after which only a 6,000-instance subsample of the resulting 90% portion was used for training because of the 4-GB GPU's training time (Section IV.1.1); it is this second, smaller subsample that yields 507 covered targets and, with the independently subsampled validation portion, the 74% figure, rather than the near-complete target overlap that a single clean instance-level split of a 52-poses-per-target pool would otherwise be expected to produce. The validation loss therefore measured interpolation among poses of partly seen targets, whereas guidance requires transfer to an unseen pocket, which only the test set measured. With 27 test pockets, however, the test numbers themselves carried large sampling uncertainty.

@sec Leakage and Coverage Audit

Before any retraining, it was checked whether the near-zero test quality, or the later null guidance results, could be explained by leakage. The audit found the following.

(i) Pocket level: the 20 target identifiers of the guidance ablation of Section IV.5 were compared with the training and validation index sets rebuilt with the true training seed, and no target was shared. Seven of these 20 targets were, however, members of the 27-pocket test set of the predictor, for which metrics (not gradients) had been computed during training; this is disclosed because it is relevant for a strict reading of "independent test".

(ii) Label provenance: the labels were experimental pKd, pKi and pIC50 values, not docking scores. This rules out the subtler leakage in which a predictor is trained on labels produced by the same docking configuration that is later used for evaluation.

(iii) Family level: only a coarse gene-symbol comparison was possible at that time. One coincidental match was found between two proteins with the same four-letter mnemonic but different organisms and folds. Because no sequence-identity clustering was available, the audit did not claim the absence of leakage at the fold-family level. The leakage-safe split of Section IV.4 was built to close this gap.

(iv) Coverage: 76,803 of the 184,087 annotated entries had a non-zero pK. The training subsample was wide but shallow, with about 11.8 examples per target.

The audit therefore found no disqualifying leakage in the sense of the guidance evaluation, and it directed the investigation toward generalisation.

@sec Hyper-Parameter Sweep and Diagnostics on the Old Split

@sub Learning rate and depth

A light sweep varied the learning rate and the depth of the EGNN, with all other settings, the data and the seed held fixed, and with a full 20-epoch budget per configuration ({tab:sweep}). Lowering the learning rate to 3 × 10⁻⁵ gave the only configuration with a positive test R² (0.046) and a clearly better test correlation (0.538) than the deployed configuration (0.233). A shallower, four-layer network had the best validation quality of all four configurations (Pearson 0.806) and the worst test quality (0.119), an illustration of how validation loss on shared-target data can select for models that transfer worse.

The improvement must, however, be read with the bootstrap intervals for n = 27. The 95% intervals of the test R² of the best configuration (−0.601 to 0.445) and of the deployed configuration (−1.769 to 0.183) overlapped, so a positive R² could not be claimed. The correlation intervals were more informative: 0.230 to 0.775 for the best configuration, which excludes zero, and −0.145 to 0.595 for the deployed one.

Two readings are supported by these diagnostics. First, on this split, a representation without any protein information was indistinguishable from the pocket-aware network, so the gap was not specific to the EGNN. Second, the predictions correlated strongly with the ligand's heavy-atom count, the same kind of shortcut that is known to inflate docking scores of generated molecules [@gao2025; @xu2022]. A third possible reading, that more training data would close the gap, was not confirmed: the learning curve was flat between 25 and 75% of the targets and rose only at 100%, but the four intervals overlapped, so the test set with 27 pockets was too small to resolve this question. At this point the investigation concluded that the gap was more consistent with data scarcity and superficial features than with an architecture flaw. This conclusion was revised in the next section.

@sec The Split Artefact: Leakage-Safe Re-Evaluation

@sub Construction of the split

The diagnostics above had been obtained on a split in which the validation set overlapped with the training set by target and the test set contained only 27 pockets. To repeat the evaluation under a defensible protocol, the target-level leakage-safe (LP) split described in Section III.2.3 was constructed. Two calibration problems were found and repaired during its construction: a rule that seeded the test set had to be bounded to avoid its runaway growth, and a ligand-identity overlap between validation and test that had passed the original algorithm was removed by an explicit check. The final split had 779 training targets (46,964 entries), 65 validation targets (6,069 entries) and 127 test targets (11,855 entries). Of the 1,002 targets carried forward into this assignment (Section III.2.3), 31 were discarded for ligand contamination: 10 on the training side and 21 where a validation target remained contaminated against the already-finalised test set, leaving 971 kept targets (779 + 65 + 127), with verified zero target overlap between any two subsets.

@sub Result

The Stage 0 EGNN configuration was retrained on this split without changing any architecture or hyper-parameter (6,000-entry training subsample, learning rate 10⁻⁴; best validation loss at epoch 4, early stopping after epoch 9). On the 11,855 test entries it reached a Pearson correlation of 0.609 (95% CI 0.597 to 0.621) and R² of 0.342 (0.322 to 0.361), as shown in {tab:lpquality} and {fig:split}. A ligand-only random forest reached R² 0.195, so on the repaired split, the pocket-aware model had a nominal advantage over the ligand-only baseline, opposite to the pattern on the old split (this difference was not tested pairwise, and the complex-level interval is optimistic; see Chapter VIII). The correlation of the predictions with the heavy-atom count decreased modestly, from 0.799 to 0.719, and remained strong.

This result contradicted the expectation stated when the split was built, namely that the split alone would not repair the model, and it contradicted the reading of the previous section. It is reported as such. A substantial part of the earlier gap was a consequence of the split construction (target-overlapping validation data and a 27-pocket test set), and not of the EGNN architecture or an unavoidable data limit. The earlier diagnostics were correct as measurements on that split, but the causal story built upon them was answering the wrong question. Two limits of this conclusion should be noted. The comparison is between models evaluated on different test sets, so the two R² values are not paired estimates; and the size shortcut (correlation 0.719) is still present, so a better-than-baseline predictor is not the same as a predictor that has learned binding chemistry.

@sec Baseline Guidance Ablations

@sub Task F: the original ablation

The first guidance ablation compared unguided sampling with affinity-guided sampling, with the original predictor at λ = 1.0. It used 20 of the 100 official test pockets (every fifth pocket in the official order, with one substitution because a receptor file was missing from the data mirror), each a distinct protein by gene symbol (the coarse family proxy available at this stage of the project; Section III.8), 3 seeds and 10 molecules per (variant, pocket, seed), giving 1,193 scored molecules (597 unguided and 596 guided; 0 failed runs). Docking used AutoDock Vina with exhaustiveness 16 against coordinate-matched receptors; an earlier attempt with receptor files from a different data mirror had produced meaningless zero-interaction scores because of a coordinate-frame mismatch, and was caught in a dry run. The value λ = 1.0 had been chosen in an earlier single-pocket sweep because it was the strongest tested value that left validity unaffected.

No metric differed significantly between the variants ({tab:taskf}); all p-values were above 0.27 and most above 0.6, for both the pooled Mann–Whitney test and the Wilcoxon test paired by pocket (n = 20). The standard deviation of the unguided Vina Dock (10.08 kcal/mol) was four times that of the guided condition (2.41), which indicated a heavy-tailed distribution produced by a few extreme poses; the paired test, which is more robust to such outliers, agreed with the unpaired test. The numerically lower guided mean Vina Dock (−7.25 against −6.81 kcal/mol) was not distinguishable from noise (p = 0.96 unpaired, 0.55 paired), and the ligand efficiency did not worsen (−0.372 against −0.340), so no signature of Vina hacking [@gao2025] was found. Because there was no significant gain, there was also no gain to be tested for authenticity.

@sub Strength re-sweep with the original predictor

Two explanations were considered for the null result: that λ = 1.0 was too weak, or that the predictor's gradient carried no useful signal. The first was tested by docking molecules generated at nine values of λ between 1 and 30 on the bundled example pocket (n = 8 per value). Fragmentation of the molecules started between λ = 20 and λ = 30 (50% single-fragment rate at λ = 30), which defined 20 as the practical safety ceiling. Within the safe range, no comparison with λ = 1.0 was significant (Mann–Whitney p between 0.79 and 1.00 for all eight comparisons), and the mean Vina Dock moved non-monotonically within a band of about 2 kcal/mol. The predictor's own score rose by only about 1.2% across the range, and this drift did not correspond to any trend in independent Vina Dock. The first explanation was therefore refuted, and the null result was not an artefact of an overly conservative operating point.

@sub Strength re-sweep with the repaired predictor

The same question was asked again after the repair of Section IV.4, because the improved predictor could have been expected to change the result. The sweep covered eight values of λ from 0 to 100, with n = 8 molecules per value. Robustness improved markedly: fragmentation appeared only at λ = 100 (one of eight molecules), against λ = 30 for the original predictor. The independent Vina Dock, however, showed no dose–response ({tab:lpresweep}); the comparison of each value against unguided sampling had p between 0.69 and 1.00.

A confirmation over eight diverse pockets (one seed, n = 8 per condition, λ = 0.3, 10 and 100, independent Vina docking with exhaustiveness 16, 32 runs and no failure) gave all 24 pocket-level comparisons as non-significant (p from 0.105 to 1.000). A paired-by-pocket Wilcoxon test at λ = 0.3 reached nominal significance (p = 0.023), but the effect was a consistent, tiny, wrong-directed shift (+0.030 kcal/mol, that is, slightly worse binding under guidance; seven of eight pockets shifting by +0.01 to +0.055), an order of magnitude below the typical per-molecule standard deviation of 1.1–1.6 kcal/mol. With six primary tests, one nominal p < 0.05 is expected by chance, and the other two λ values showed no pattern (p = 0.547 and 0.742). It was reported for completeness, and was not treated as evidence that guidance works.

@sec A Fuller Information-Content Audit: What Representation Predicts Affinity?

The comparison of {tab:lpquality} established that a ligand-only random forest on ECFP4 fingerprints (R² 0.195) did not match the EGNN (0.342). This section asks the same question with a wider and, as it turns out, more damaging set of ligand-only representations, together with every other feature block that could plausibly be swapped in as a guidance signal: pocket-only descriptors, raw and size-normalised contact-count fingerprints (ECIF), and the typed non-covalent interaction counts (PLIP) considered as a guidance representation in Chapter III. All comparisons below use the leakage-safe split of Section IV.4, target-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals (B = 2,000) and Benjamini–Hochberg correction across every pairwise comparison reported together, following the statistical protocol of Section III.6.

@sub The representation ladder

{tab:ladder} reports the held-out R² of eight representation blocks, each paired with the model (ridge regression or a gradient-boosted tree) that performed best on it in a light hyper-parameter search. A 14-descriptor ligand-only block (molecular weight, logP, topological polar surface area, rotatable-bond count and ten related RDKit descriptors, no protein information at all) reaches R² = 0.3531, matching the EGNN's 0.3420 (single seed) and 0.3437 (three-seed mean, SD 0.0377). A single feature, the ligand's heavy-atom count alone, reaches R² = 0.3069, 90% of the structure-aware model's variance explained. A 38-descriptor pocket-only block, with no ligand information and evaluated on unseen targets, reaches R² = 0.2889.

@sub Ligand size, not interaction identity, is the dominant signal

Three findings converge. First, combining ligand and pocket information produced no gain over either source alone (ligand+pocket vs. ligand: tie; vs. pocket: tie, after correction), so the two sources are largely redundant rather than complementary on this split. Second, the raw ECIF contact-count fingerprint (0.3701) significantly outperformed its own size-normalised variant (0.2077; ΔR² = +0.1625, q = 0.013), indicating that most of its apparent signal is a re-encoding of ligand size through contact *count* rather than contact *identity*; a specific prior claim that a size-normalised interaction fingerprint of this kind should retain the relevant signal (the CORDIAL finding of Brown et al., reviewed in Chapter II) was not supported on this split, since the normalised fingerprint tied with both ligand-only and pocket-only marginals rather than exceeding them.

Third, and sharper still, the same pattern holds for a representation that is chemically explicit by design rather than only implicitly size-correlated. Four typed non-covalent interaction counts (hydrogen bond, hydrophobic contact, π-stacking and salt bridge, extracted with PLIP and summed per complex, the coarsest possible typed-interaction representation and the one considered for guidance in Chapter III) explain almost no variance on their own (R² 0.07–0.08) and lose significantly to both the ligand-only block (ΔR² = −0.2712, q = 0.003) and the pocket-only block (ΔR² = −0.2066, q = 0.003). Unlike the ECIF pair, the raw and size-normalised PLIP counts did not differ from each other (ΔR² = −0.0080, q = 0.870), so this particular block carries no detectable confound between its raw and normalised forms considered alone; what moves the result is adding a single explicit size covariate (heavy-atom count) to the four interaction counts, which raises R² from 0.0822 to 0.3241 (ΔR² = +0.2419, q < 0.001). A representation built specifically to describe *what kind* of contact a ligand makes is, on this split, dominated by *how big* the ligand is to a degree comparable to, and in this instance sharper than, the simpler ECIF count fingerprint.

@sub Structure rarely beats ligand-only, after correction

A systematic pairwise comparison of every representation block against every other, 40 comparisons in total with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, found 12 cases where a structure-aware block significantly outperformed a ligand-only block, 28 ties, and zero cases where a ligand-only block significantly outperformed a structure-aware one. Three nominally significant wins for structure (p = 0.039, 0.044 and 0.049 before correction) did not survive the correction (q = 0.12–0.13) and are reported as ties. This is consistent with, and sharpens, the single comparison of {tab:lpquality}: on a target-disjoint, leakage-safe split, no representation of the binding pocket tested in this thesis, including a chemically typed interaction representation, reliably outperforms a 14-descriptor model of the ligand alone.

@sec Does More Training Data Close the Gap? Augmenting with BindingNet

The predictor examined so far was trained only on CrossDocked2020. A separate question is whether its ceiling is a property of the architecture and representation, which the previous section addresses, or simply of training-set size and diversity. BindingNet is a larger, structurally paired affinity data set; this section reports a dedicated training-data-composition study (Arms 0, B, A and C) that augments the CrossDocked training pool with BindingNet rows and asks whether the resulting predictor closes the distance to the ligand-only ceiling (0.3531) or exceeds the class-level prior established in Section IV.8.

@sub A compound-level leak, found and removed before it could inflate a result

Before the production arms were run, a compound-level leakage audit of the combined CrossDocked–BindingNet pool found that 423 of 124,973 candidate training rows (0.34%) shared a ligand, by exact structure match, with a row in the validation or test partition. Measured the other way, the affected ligands accounted for 10.12% of evaluation records (1,814 of 17,924) and 8.6% of distinct validation/test ligands, a thirty-fold difference between the two ways of expressing the same leak that illustrates why a leakage audit must be read from the evaluation side, not only from the training side, to judge its practical severity. A seed-by-seed replay of the exact sampling used for an earlier exploratory run (Arm B, seed 2021) found actual per-seed exposure of 3.06–4.65% of evaluation records across three seeds. A filter was built and applied before any production arm was trained, removing the 423 rows (123 distinct compounds) and is enforced in code, not only upstream in a cached file, by `train_surrogate_arm.py`'s `compound_filter=True` default, which raises explicitly if its reference summary file is missing.

@sub Arm A versus Arm C: pure CrossDocked outperforms BindingNet augmentation at matched size

Two arms, each with three seeds, were trained under the batch-size-1, no-mixed-precision regime validated in Section III.7 (an earlier batch-size-4, mixed-precision configuration was rejected at an explicit quality gate, Section III.7.2, because of oscillating validation R² and markedly worse final quality). Arm A trained on 20,000 CrossDocked rows only. Arm C trained on the production composition (6,000 CrossDocked plus 14,000 BindingNet rows, matched to the total row count of Arm A) drawn from the leakage-filtered pool. {tab:armac} reports the result, alongside the earlier Arm B-match run (the same composition as Arm C, but from the pool *before* the compound filter existed, included as a check of the filter's practical effect).

Two readings follow, both stated with the appropriate caveat. First, Arm A's CI95 [0.299, 0.425] comes close to, but does not clearly exceed, the ligand-only ceiling of 0.3531; Arm C's CI95 [0.224, 0.320] does not reach the ligand-only ceiling at all, and its lower bound sits below the point estimate of the class-level prior established in the next section (0.2468), so Arm C cannot be confidently distinguished from a predictor that has learned no more than the typical affinity of a target's protein class. Augmenting the training pool with BindingNet rows, even after removing the compound-level leak, did not close the distance to the ligand-only ceiling at the row counts tested here, and the pure-CrossDocked arm of matched total size performed descriptively better. Second, the compound-level filter itself had little visible effect once applied (Arm C 0.272 against the unfiltered Arm B-match's 0.278, heavily overlapping CIs): the leak identified above does not appear to have been propping up the earlier Arm B result by a large margin, which is a reassuring, if secondary, methodological finding, even though removing the leak remained the correct decision on principle regardless of its measured impact.

@sec How Much of the Predictor's Performance Is a Class-Level Prior?

A model can appear to read a protein target's binding pocket while, in fact, reading only the target's class membership: a ligand's chemotype can correlate with the typical affinity range of the protein family it was designed against, without the model having learned anything about structure–activity relationships within that target. y-Randomisation, a standard QSAR control against chance correlation [@rucker2007], was adapted here to isolate this effect specifically: labels are permuted *within* each target, so the per-target label multiset (and with it, the target's typical affinity range) is preserved, while which ligand carries which label is destroyed. A model that still performs well under this control is reading class membership, not within-target structure–activity relationship.

@sub Two baselines under within-target permutation

A global permutation, which destroys all structure in the labels, served as a first check: the 14-descriptor ligand-only ridge reached a mean R² of only 0.0037 (max 0.0141) under 20 global permutations, far below its real-label performance of 0.3531, confirming that the real-label result is not an artefact of an overly flexible 14-feature model. Under within-target permutation, however, the same ridge model reached R² = 0.2468 (SD 0.0019 across 20 permutations): **70% of its real-label performance is reproducible with no within-target signal at all.** The same control was then run on the deployed EGNN architecture itself, not only on the descriptor pipeline, by retraining it under its exact Stage 0 hyperparameters and split with training labels permuted within target: it reached R² = 0.307 (Pearson 0.560), compared with 0.342 (Pearson 0.609) for the same architecture trained on real labels. **The EGNN reproduces 90% of its own real-label performance (0.307 / 0.342) without any within-target structure–activity signal**, a larger fraction than the descriptor baseline's 70%, and by a more direct mechanism: the architecture reads 3-D target geometry at the input, so it can recognise which target it is looking at without needing the indirect chemotype correlation that supplies the descriptor model's class prior.

Read from the correct zero point, that is, from the class-level prior rather than from R² = 0, the structure-aware ensemble of Chapter III's uncertainty suite accounts for 48.5% of the remaining headroom above the class prior, a 14-descriptor ligand-only ridge for 32.1%, and the original Vina docking score for only 4.0%. The window within which any of these models' true performance can be distinguished, however, is itself narrow: the gap between the floor (the ligand-only ceiling, 0.3531) and the label-noise ceiling (0.5776) spans only 0.2245 R², which is narrower than the average width of a single target-clustered bootstrap confidence interval measured for this task (0.3871; ratio 0.58). A single comparison reporting a gain of 0.02–0.05 R² against a similarly sized benchmark, a magnitude common in the literature reviewed in Chapter II, cannot in principle resolve where within this window a model sits. This same ceiling is why the argument made earlier in this thesis, that the Stage 0 predictor's moderate correlation (Pearson 0.58–0.65) rules out a trivial explanation for the guidance failures of Chapters V to VII, needs qualification: a model with no protein input at all reaches a comparable correlation by the class-prior route alone, so correlation magnitude on its own does not establish that the predictor has learned pocket-specific structure–activity relationships. This qualification is returned to in Chapter IX.

The labels underlying this entire decomposition are themselves heterogeneous: 37% Kd, 37% IC50 and 26% Ki measurements, and this composition shifts between splits (41% Kd / 33% IC50 in training against 46% IC50 / 26% Kd in test), with IC50 the most assay-condition-dependent of the three and over-represented in the test partition. This is disclosed as a further source of irreducible variance contributing to the label-noise ceiling above, not as a new finding.

@sec Where Does the Predictor Fail? Activity Cliffs, Matched Pairs and Protein Families

The sections above describe what the predictor reads on average. This section asks where it fails specifically, using three standard diagnostics from the QSAR literature that none of this project's earlier chapters had applied: activity cliffs, matched molecular pairs and stratification by protein family.

@sub Activity cliffs

Following the convention of the MoleculeACE benchmark [@vantilborg2022] (ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity ≥ 0.9, |Δ log-affinity| ≥ 1.0, computed within target only, since the leakage-safe split is target-disjoint and cross-target pairs are not meaningful here), 2,856 activity-cliff pairs were found among 4,050,366 within-target pairs checked across the 11,855 test ligands and 127 test targets, involving 344 distinct compounds (2.9% of the test set). On this cliff stratum, the ligand-only model's R² (0.3611) nominally exceeded the structure-aware model's (0.2904), the pattern reported for a comparable benchmark in the literature [@vantilborg2022], but the paired, target-clustered difference (ΔR² = −0.0708, 95% CI [−0.428, 0.065]) did not exclude zero (p = 0.285) and is not reported as a significant finding; on the much larger non-cliff stratum (11,511 pairs' worth of ligands, 126 targets), the structure-aware model's nominal advantage (ΔR² = +0.0594) was likewise not significant (95% CI [−0.032, 0.150]). Neither stratum resolves a winner at this sample size; the honest reading is that activity cliffs do not provide additional evidence either way beyond the aggregate comparison of Section IV.8.3.

@sub Matched molecular pairs

Single-cut matched-molecular-pair fragmentation (the standard Hussain–Rea algorithm, applied within target only, requiring the shared core to be at least 60% of molecular weight) found 183 matched series, 375 pairs, 360 with a non-zero affinity difference. Sign agreement with the true direction of the affinity change, the most local and in principle the easiest structure–activity signal a model could capture [@kwapien2022], was 0.461 for the structure-aware ensemble and 0.483 for the ligand-only ridge, both at or below the 0.5 expected by chance (95% CI [0.399, 0.535] and [0.391, 0.596] respectively, both including 0.5). A paired bootstrap of the difference between the two models on the identical pairs (−0.0222, 95% CI [−0.102, 0.043], p = 0.551) found no significant difference between them. Neither model reliably predicts the direction of the simplest, most local structure–activity relationship this thesis tested, a finding consistent with, and more severe than, the class-prior decomposition of Section IV.8: performance across this project's predictors does not appear to derive from structure–activity capture even at the most local level achievable.

@sub Protein-family stratification

Stratifying the same ligand-only-versus-structure comparison by protein family (Pfam annotation, available for all 127 test targets) found only two of ten family strata with the ten-target-cluster minimum this thesis requires before reporting a verdict: the kinase family PF00069 (11 targets, 3,306 ligands) and a combined "other family" stratum (83 targets, 6,751 ligands); the remaining seven families (3–7 targets each) are marked underpowered rather than given a verdict, because their point estimates varied wildly (one early draft of this analysis printed a spurious ΔR² of +5.8 × 10¹⁶ for a six-row family whose local label variance was close to, but not exactly, zero, a bug fixed with an explicit relative-variance guard before any result was reported from it). Within the two powered strata, neither showed a significant structure-versus-ligand-only difference after correction (kinase: ΔR² = +0.0747, 95% CI [−0.264, 0.365], q = 0.652; other families combined: ΔR² = +0.0459, 95% CI [−0.068, 0.154], q = 0.652). Even within the single largest, practically most consequential family tested, kinases, structure-aware prediction does not significantly outperform a ligand-only baseline, consistent with the aggregate finding of Section IV.8.3 holding down to the family level where it can be tested at all.

@sec Summary of the Chapter

Four results of this chapter carry forward. First, an apparent generalisation gap of the affinity predictor was in large part a split artefact: after moving to a target-disjoint split of adequate size, the same architecture explained 34% of test variance ({fig:quality}), with an interval that excluded zero and a nominal margin over a weak ligand-only baseline (0.20). This margin did not survive a fuller audit: against a stronger 14-descriptor ligand-only model, the structure-aware predictor is statistically indistinguishable (0.3531 against 0.3420–0.3437), and no representation of the pocket tested in this thesis, including a chemically typed interaction representation, reliably beat ligand-only after correction for multiple comparisons. Second, the size shortcut identified early in this chapter (correlation 0.72 with heavy-atom count) generalises: it reappears, in a sharper form, in a chemically explicit interaction representation, where adding one size covariate to four typed interaction counts more than quadruples the explained variance. Third, 70% (descriptor pipeline) to 90% (the deployed EGNN itself) of the predictor's apparent performance is reproducible from a class-level prior alone, under a within-target label permutation that destroys all genuine structure–activity relationship; what remains after removing this prior is a narrow window, smaller than a single confidence interval on this task, and neither activity cliffs nor matched molecular pairs nor the largest single protein family tested provide evidence that any predictor in this thesis captures local structure–activity relationship within it. A dedicated attempt to close this gap by augmenting training data with a larger external affinity data set (BindingNet) did not succeed at matched training-set size, after a compound-level leak in the combined pool was found and removed. Fourth, and decisive for the rest of the thesis, repairing the predictor was necessary but not sufficient: with the repaired predictor as with the original one, guidance produced no detectable change in the independently computed docking score at any tested strength (a span of three orders of magnitude for the repaired predictor). Guidance also changed the predictor's own score only marginally, by 1 to 2%. Three questions followed: whether a differently constructed predictor or a differently constructed guidance mechanism could recover a signal (Chapter V), whether the same predictor used without a gradient, as a ranker of finished molecules, would do better (Chapter VI), and, once both of those also failed, whether the failure traced to the signal or the mechanism, a question Chapter VI.5 answers directly with a positive control built from a genuine, non-surrogate affinity signal.
