@ch II | LITERATURE REVIEW

This chapter reviews the literature on which the experimental chapters build. It proceeds from the generative models that are guided, through the predictors that supply the guidance signal and the pitfalls in evaluating both, to the two methodological ideas (residual learning and interaction supervision) that motivate the final experiment. Throughout, statements are limited to what the cited sources report. Where a body of work could not be examined in full, this is stated in the text.

@sec Deep Generative Models for Structure-Based Drug Design

The generation of ligands conditioned on a protein pocket has been approached with several model families. Early voxel-grid and autoregressive atom-placement models were followed by non-autoregressive models based on denoising diffusion [@ho2020], which learn to reverse a gradual noising process. TargetDiff [@guan2023] applies an equivariant diffusion model to the joint distribution of ligand atom coordinates and atom types conditioned on a protein pocket, and additionally uses the learned representation for affinity prediction. It is trained on the CrossDocked2020 data set [@francoeur2020], which contains 22.5 million poses of ligands docked into multiple similar binding pockets of the Protein Data Bank. Because CrossDocked2020 was produced by cross-docking, the poses are docking outputs rather than experimental complexes, a property whose consequences for evaluation are discussed in Section II.4.

Equivariant graph networks [@satorras2021] provide the architectural basis for such models: node features are updated from invariant quantities such as distances, and coordinates are updated along relative position vectors, so that rotating or translating the complex rotates or translates the output. The same family of networks is used in this thesis for the affinity predictor that supplies guidance (Chapter III).

Recent work has attempted to inject interaction knowledge into 3D generators. DeepICL conditions an autoregressive generator on atom-level interaction labels of four types (hydrogen bond, salt bridge, hydrophobic and π-stacking) extracted from reference structures with the protein–ligand interaction profiler PLIP during training, and reports improved docking scores against a baseline without interaction information [@zhung2024]. InterDiff conditions a diffusion model on interaction "prompts" detected with BINANA, uses cross-attention to inject them, and adds a cross-entropy loss that classifies protein-atom features into interaction types inside the denoiser [@wu2024]. BInD treats non-covalent interactions extracted with PLIP as an additional categorical channel that is generated jointly with atoms and bonds under its own loss, and applies knowledge-based guidance terms after the network prediction [@lee2025bind]. An earlier recurrent generator was constrained by an interaction fingerprint computed on docking poses [@zhang2022ifp]. DPDiff, a diffusion model on CrossDocked2020, extracts "interaction priors" from two pretrained affinity-prediction networks during sampling, using intermediate diffusion predictions as input [@huang2026]. Its ablation reports that a geometry-based prior that was pretrained on accurate poses degraded generation when queried on noisy intermediate structures, because of the mismatch between training and sampling inputs [@huang2026]. This observation is directly relevant to guidance and is returned to in Chapters V and IX.

@sec Guidance of Diffusion Models

Classifier guidance modifies the reverse process of a diffusion model with the gradient of the log-probability of a target class under an auxiliary classifier evaluated on noisy inputs, thereby trading diversity for fidelity to the class [@dhariwal2021]. In molecular generation, the same principle has been applied with regressors of properties such as affinity. Two design decisions distinguish implementations. First, the guidance predictor may be trained on noisy inputs at every diffusion time, or on clean structures and then evaluated on the model's clean-data estimate. The latter avoids retraining and is cheaper, but the predictor then receives inputs that differ from its training distribution, particularly at early, noisy steps. Second, the guidance may act on continuous coordinates only, or also on discrete atom types; discrete types require a differentiable relaxation and, for predictors that use bond-derived features, an estimate of connectivity that does not exist during sampling. Both decisions are made explicitly in Chapter III, and the second is examined empirically in Chapter VII.

The approach closest to the gradient-guidance routes of this thesis is BADGER, whose classifier-guidance variant applies the gradient of a separately trained affinity predictor to a frozen diffusion SBDD generator at sampling time, in essentially the same plug-and-play arrangement as Chapters IV to VI, and reports up to a 60% improvement in the binding affinity of sampled molecules [@jian2026badger]; its companion classifier-free variant instead folds affinity conditioning into the generator's own training, which this thesis's frozen-generator design does not attempt. DiffGui integrates bond diffusion with affinity and drug-likeness guidance trained jointly with the generator, rather than through a separately frozen predictor, and reports improved binding affinity together with validation on a small set of wet-laboratory assays [@hu2025diffgui]. Neither paper's abstract or summary materials available to this search state whether the reported affinity improvement was evaluated on a target-disjoint split, tested for statistical significance with correction for multiple comparisons, or checked against a negative control or a quality-degradation criterion (ligand efficiency, molecular size, physical validity) of the kind used throughout this thesis; this search did not extend to their full methods sections, and no claim is made here about what those sections do or do not report (Appendix B). Section IX.5 returns to the apparent tension between their reported positive result and this thesis's null result once the full evidence is in.

@sec Predicting Binding Affinity

@sub Docking scores and their biases

AutoDock Vina combines an empirical scoring function with efficient conformational search and has been extended in later releases with additional features and Python bindings [@trott2010; @eberhardt2021]. Its score is reported in kcal/mol and, being fast, is used ubiquitously both to evaluate generated molecules and as a label or baseline for machine-learned scoring. Its limitations are well documented. In a systematic analysis of docking successes and failures on the DUD-E benchmark, the Vina scoring function was reported to show a bias toward compounds of higher molecular weight [@xu2022]. Re-parameterisations of the function, such as Vinardo, were introduced because the conventional approach of fitting empirical terms by linear regression to experimental affinities does not yield a function with optimal docking capabilities [@quiroga2016]. The choice of docking-based baseline therefore matters, a point that becomes central to the delta-learning experiment of Chapter VIII.

@sub Learned scoring functions

Machine-learned scoring functions have been built on convolutional and graph architectures. GIGN unifies covalent and non-covalent interactions in one heterogeneous message-passing layer that is invariant to rotations and translations of the complex, and reports state-of-the-art performance on three external test sets [@yang2023gign]. PIGNet predicts atom–atom pairwise interaction terms with physics-informed equations whose parameters are produced by a neural network and sums them to obtain the affinity, and augments the training data with additional poses and ligands to improve generalisation [@moon2022]. Interformer combines a graph-transformer with an interaction-aware mixture density network for docking and affinity prediction [@lai2024], and PLANET is trained with three objectives (affinity, protein–ligand contact map and ligand distance matrix) [@zhang2024planet]. A framework that adds an adjustable affinity term to the training of a mixture density network was proposed to balance scoring, docking, ranking and screening powers, which the authors note are rarely achieved simultaneously by a single machine-learned function [@shen2023]. The GIGN layers and the PIGNet physics decomposition are the two architectural ingredients of the physics-anchored predictor examined in Chapter V.

@sub Data leakage and shortcut learning

A large part of the recent literature concerns the reliability of the reported performance of these models. A study with modular graph networks showed that an explicit description of protein–ligand non-covalent interactions provided no advantage over ligand or protein descriptors alone, and that simple models that infer the affinity of a test sample from the closest ligands or proteins in the training set already performed well, which the authors interpreted as memorisation dominating true learning [@volkov2022]. Retraining top models on PDBbind CleanSplit, which removes structure-based train–test leakage and redundancy within the training set, caused their benchmark performance to drop substantially, indicating that reported performance was largely driven by leakage [@graber2025]. The Leak Proof PDBBind data set re-organises PDBbind so that proteins and ligands with high sequence and structural similarity do not cross the split, and reports that models retrained on it perform better on a new independent set [@li2026lp]. Complementary evidence comes from work in which the injection of additional geometric descriptors into a graph network gave no statistically significant improvement on leak-controlled splits [@balci2026], and from a scoring function whose attributions were compared with a distance-based interaction profiler in order to test whether it had learned interactions rather than data-set biases [@scantlebury2023]. The leakage-safe, target-level split used in this thesis (Chapter III) follows the spirit of these studies; it is an adaptation of the Leak Proof PDBBind procedure to CrossDocked2020 and not the published data set itself.

@sec Evaluating Generated Molecules

The reliability of docking scores as the success criterion for generative models has been questioned directly. A study that proposed practical alternative metrics reported that Vina scores can be inflated simply by increasing the number of atoms in a molecule, and that current SBDD models with high Vina scores fall short on measures of practical usability [@gao2024arxiv; @gao2025]. In its published (ICLR 2025) version, the same work states that "some models use trained Vina predictors to guide sampling, prioritizing the hacking of Vina scores rather than generating reliable and actual effective molecules", and that "many existing models use the test set as a validation set to select checkpoints during training, which risks data leakage" [@gao2025]. It also observes that the structures in CrossDocked2020 are generated by docking software rather than being real complexes, and that the selection of the data relies on docking software, so that the ligands chosen for training tend to be favoured by the docking software [@gao2025]. That study defines a "delta score" as the mean difference between the docking score of a molecule against a randomly sampled other target and against its own target, i.e. a measure of specificity [@gao2025; @ren2023; @gao2024sbe]. This quantity is unrelated to the residual-against-docking-score target ("delta-learning") used in Chapter VIII, and the shared name is a terminological collision that is disambiguated wherever it arises in this thesis.

Physical plausibility is an independent axis of evaluation. PoseBusters is a suite of chemical and geometric checks (stereochemistry, planarity of aromatic rings, bond lengths, protein–ligand clashes) which showed that several deep-learning docking methods frequently produce physically implausible poses even when their root-mean-square deviation from the native pose is low [@buttenschoen2024]. A related study of interaction recovery reported that ignoring interaction fingerprints can lead to an overestimation of model performance, notably for co-folding models that often fail to recapitulate key interactions [@errington2025]. Together, these works motivate the multi-criterion evaluation harness of Chapter III, in which a docking score is never interpreted without a size-normalised measure (ligand efficiency) and a physical-validity check.

@sec Estimating Synthesizability

Two families of synthesizability estimates are in common use. The synthetic accessibility score (SA score) combines fragment contributions derived from one million PubChem molecules with a complexity penalty and maps a molecule to a value between 1 (easy) and 10 (very difficult) [@ertl2009]. The retrosynthetic accessibility score (RAscore) is a machine-learned classifier that predicts whether a synthetic route can be found for a compound by the computer-aided synthesis planning tool AiZynthFinder, and is at least 4500 times faster than the underlying planning [@thakkar2021]. In this thesis, the RAscore is used as the target of the synthesizability guidance predictor and, independently, as an evaluation metric computed with the reference implementation (Chapters III and VII). A pre-screening tool for enumerated and generated compounds, it does not by itself certify that a route exists.

@sec Residual (Delta) Learning for Scoring

Delta-learning trains a model to predict the difference between a high-quality target and a cheap baseline, so that the baseline supplies the bulk of the prediction. It was introduced for quantum chemistry, where machine-learning corrections added to approximate legacy methods reproduce high-level enthalpies of large molecular sets after training on 1–10% of the data [@ramakrishnan2015], and it remains in use, for example for correcting a semiempirical method in the prediction of reaction barrier heights [@kautish2026]. For protein–ligand scoring, ΔVinaRF20 adds a random forest with 20 descriptors to the Vina score; its authors report that direct random-forest scoring functions gain in scoring power but lose docking and screening power relative to classical functions, whereas the delta formulation was superior in all power tests of two Comparative Assessment of Scoring Functions (CASF) benchmarks [@wang2017]. Related work re-scored Vina poses with random forests [@li2015], and a delta model built on a linear empirical baseline and gradient boosting reported Pearson correlations of 0.853, 0.839 and 0.813 on locally optimised, redocked and ensemble-docked poses of the CASF-2016 core set [@yang2022]. All of these use tree ensembles on hand-crafted features and are evaluated on the CASF core set, which the leakage literature of Section II.3.3 shows to overlap with common training data. To the best of the author's knowledge, a matched comparison of a residual target against an absolute target for the same graph neural network on a leakage-controlled split was not found in the literature searched; the search strategy and its limits are documented in Appendix B.

@sec Multi-Task Learning and Interaction Supervision

Multi-task training with auxiliary objectives is a common way of regularising a main task. Its central practical difficulty is balancing the losses. Uncertainty weighting learns per-task weights from homoscedastic uncertainty [@kendall2018]; gradient normalisation equalises gradient magnitudes at a shared layer [@chen2018]; gradient surgery projects a task gradient off the gradient of a conflicting task [@yu2020]; and dual balancing acts on both loss scales and gradient magnitudes [@lin2025]. Auxiliary tasks can also cause negative transfer, for which dedicated remedies have been proposed [@jiang2023], and in molecular graph networks auxiliary learning combined with gradient surgery has been used to counter it [@dey2024]. Multi-task learning has also been surveyed specifically for drug design [@allenspach2024].

Interaction supervision requires a source of interaction labels. PLIP detects seven types of non-covalent interaction at single-atom level from a three-dimensional complex, accepts custom complexes, for example from docking, and is designed for high-throughput use [@salentin2015; @adasme2021; @schake2025]. Alternative encodings include the interaction fingerprints of ProLIF [@bouysset2021] and of related tools [@wojcikowski2019; @fassio2022]. Whether such labels are reliable on poses that are not experimental is not settled: predicted poses can differ from experimental ones in their interaction profiles [@errington2025], and the stability of PLIP labels under coordinate perturbation was not characterised in the literature found, which is why it was measured in this thesis (Chapter VIII).

@sec Positioning and Research Gap

{tab:lit} summarises how the closest bodies of work relate to the experiments of this thesis. Interaction information has been used to *condition* or *co-generate* with 3D generators [@zhung2024; @lee2025bind; @huang2026], and, in one case, as an auxiliary classification loss inside the generator's own denoiser [@wu2024]. Residual learning has been applied to scoring with tree ensembles [@wang2017; @yang2022]. Multi-objective affinity networks with structural auxiliary targets exist [@zhang2024planet]. Gradient-based affinity guidance of a diffusion SBDD generator has been reported, with a positive result, by BADGER [@jian2026badger]; a jointly trained, non-frozen variant with the same guidance aim is DiffGui [@hu2025diffgui]. Neither was found, within the limits of the search described in Appendix B, to report whether its result was evaluated on a target-disjoint split, tested with correction for multiple comparisons, or checked against a negative control, although this search did not extend to their full methods and no claim is made that these checks are absent from them. What was not found, within the limits of the searches described in Appendix B, is a study that (i) evaluates learned affinity or synthesizability guidance for a 3D diffusion generator against independently computed outcomes under a leakage-safe, statistically corrected protocol with negative controls, or (ii) combines a residual target against a docking score with typed interaction supervision on a separate guidance predictor whose gradient steers the generator. The first gap motivates Chapters IV to VII; the second motivates the design of Chapter VIII, in which only the residual-target component was ultimately tested.
